This chapter describes the implementation and experimental pipeline of the thesis.
The first part covers data generation (the biological toy model and the notion of histories) and how NCA-style models are trained and evaluated.
The central part is devoted to the \emph{controlled neighborhood sweep} (Step~3): a protocol designed to isolate the effect of spatial neighborhood size \(NB\) on long-horizon behavior, with detailed metrics, robustness analysis, and non-parametric statistical tests.
The final part presents a second experiment, \emph{curriculum learning} on 500-step trajectories, to confirm that the neighborhood-size effect carries over to a long-horizon regime; it also discusses why generated trajectories can diverge from ground truth and outlines possible improvements.

%----------------------------------------------------------
\section{How experiments are structured}
%----------------------------------------------------------

The experimental work in this thesis is organized as a sequence of increasingly controlled studies.
The main research question is whether increasing the \emph{spatial neighborhood size} (\(NB\)) in Mixture Neural Cellular Automata improves long-horizon behavior in a tissue-growth setting.
In practice, the experiments are implemented as training/evaluation pipelines that can be repeated across multiple neighborhood sizes, while keeping all other hyperparameters fixed whenever possible.

At a high level, each experiment follows the same steps:
\begin{itemize}
\item generate or load a dataset of tissue-growth trajectories (``histories'');
\item train one or more NCA variants on short temporal windows extracted from these histories;
\item evaluate the trained models by running free rollouts (no teacher forcing) for different horizons;
\item compute quantitative metrics and inspect qualitative rollouts.
\end{itemize}

%----------------------------------------------------------
\section{Data generation and biological toy model}
\label{sec:impl:data}
%----------------------------------------------------------

Before training NCAs, we need supervised trajectories representing plausible tissue dynamics.
In this thesis, these trajectories are generated by a simple stochastic, biologically-inspired simulator and collected through a small data-generation pipeline.

\paragraph{State representation (cell types).}
Each simulation state is a 2D grid of discrete cell types.
In the ``complex'' configuration used for most experiments, the simulator includes a small differentiation hierarchy (e.g., \texttt{STEM}, \texttt{INTERMEDIATE}, and \texttt{DIFFERENTIATED} types), plus an \texttt{EMPTY} background.

\paragraph{Local update rules (biological intuition).}
At each simulation step, each occupied cell can:
\begin{itemize}
  \item die,
  \item survive, or
  \item divide into a nearby empty site.
\end{itemize}
When a division happens, the newly created cell may \emph{differentiate} according to a probability table that depends on the parent cell type and (optionally) on neighbor context.
This yields trajectories that show growth, spatial organization, and gradual maturation into differentiated compartments.

\paragraph{Histories.}
For a single simulation we record the entire time series of grids \(x_0, x_1, \dots, x_T\).
We refer to this time series as a \emph{history}.
In the thesis we may visualize a history either as a sequence of snapshots (selected time points) or as a small montage, because it helps identify qualitative behaviors (stable growth, collapse, frozen patterns, etc.).

\paragraph{Dataset definition.}
Formally, the dataset is a collection of histories:
\[
\mathcal{D}=\{x^{(i)}_{0:T}\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]
where each \(x^{(i)}_t\) is a discrete 2D grid state at time \(t\).
In this work we use multiple datasets with different \(N\) and \(T\) depending on the experiment.

\paragraph{How a single history is generated (example).}
To generate one history, we start from an initial condition where a small cluster of stem-like cells is placed near the center of the grid (with small randomness), while all other sites are empty.
Then, for each time step, cells update locally:
each occupied site may die, survive, or divide into a nearby empty site; if a division occurs, the newly created cell may differentiate according to a type-dependent probability table that can also be influenced by the local neighborhood composition.

The goal is not to perfectly match biology, but to produce trajectories with growth and spatial organization that can serve as supervised targets for learning a local update rule.

Different experiments used different history datasets.

\section{Model training: from trajectories to learned update rules}
%----------------------------------------------------------

All NCA-style models in this thesis learn a \emph{local} update rule that is applied repeatedly over time.
Conceptually, a standard formulation is:
\begin{equation}
x_{t+1} = x_t + f_{\theta}\!\left(\mathcal{P}(x_t)\right),
\end{equation}
where \(x_t\) is the grid state at time \(t\), \(\mathcal{P}(\cdot)\) is a local perception operator (it extracts neighborhood information), and \(f_{\theta}\) is a neural network that predicts an update.
Mixture models extend this idea by having multiple candidate update rules and a learned mechanism to select (or mix) them over space and time \cite{mnca_milite2025}.

In training, the models are usually optimized on short temporal windows extracted from longer histories (teacher-forced supervision), while evaluation is performed as free-running rollouts.
This gap is important when we compare neighborhood sizes, because a low short-horizon loss does not automatically imply stable long-horizon behavior.

\paragraph{Example of generation by the learned model (multi-step rollout).}
After training, we can use a model to generate a trajectory by starting from an initial grid \(x_0\) and iterating the learned update rule for multiple steps, producing \(\hat{x}_1, \hat{x}_2, \dots, \hat{x}_T\).
Figure~\ref{fig:impl:model:rollout_example} shows an example rollout over multiple steps (same initial condition as \cref{fig:impl:data:history_example}), illustrating what it means for the model to ``free-run''.

\section{Reference experimental setting from the original MNCA paper}
%----------------------------------------------------------

The starting point for our experiments is the training protocol used in the reference MNCA paper \cite{mnca_milite2025}.
In its ``paper-like'' regime, training uses a relatively small dataset and a short temporal horizon, with a long optimization schedule.
We replicate this setting as closely as possible and then change only the neighborhood size \(NB\), to isolate its effect.

%----------------------------------------------------------
\section{Experiment: does it make sense to extend the neighborhood?}
%----------------------------------------------------------

Our first experiment had a simple goal: understand whether extending the spatial neighborhood in MNCA is a meaningful direction.
We initially followed the paper-like regime, and then progressively changed the data and training schedule to strengthen supervision and make long-horizon training feasible.
Overall, the experiment was carried out in three steps (three configurations).

\subsection{Summary of steps 1 and 2}

We started from the training setup used in the reference paper: a relatively small dataset (\textbf{200 simulated trajectories}) with a short temporal horizon (\textbf{35 time steps}) trained for \textbf{800 epochs}.
Our first goal was not to change the learning procedure itself, but to isolate the effect of the \emph{spatial neighborhood size} (NB) by repeating the same training protocol across multiple neighborhoods.
In principle, increasing NB should allow the update rule to condition on a broader spatial context, which could improve regeneration and pattern stability.

In practice, this ``paper-like'' regime did not produce a clean answer to the neighborhood question.
Across several NB values, the qualitative rollouts remained inconsistent and the quantitative differences were small, making it hard to tell whether:
\begin{itemize}
  \item NB genuinely had little impact, or
  \item the training signal was simply too weak/noisy in this low-data, short-horizon setting.
\end{itemize}
At that point, we were not confident that a negative result would be meaningful.

Following this, we attempted to strengthen the supervision by increasing both dataset size and sequence length, moving to \textbf{1000 trajectories of 500 time steps} each.
The motivation was twofold:
\begin{itemize}
  \item more trajectories reduce variance and improve coverage of initial conditions;
  \item longer sequences better represent the long-horizon behavior we ultimately care about when judging whether a model ``regenerates'' rather than just matching short-term transitions.
\end{itemize}
However, training directly on long sequences at this scale was computationally heavy and slowed iteration substantially.

To make long-horizon training feasible, we introduced \textbf{curriculum learning}, training on progressively longer temporal windows (e.g., a first phase with a \textbf{35-step} window, then \textbf{100-step}, and finally \textbf{500-step}, with fewer epochs and smaller learning rates in later phases).
The intended effect of the curriculum was to let the models first learn stable short-term local dynamics and then refine them under longer-horizon constraints without paying the full cost of long unrolls from the beginning.

Despite the curriculum, the resulting rollouts were often poor (the tissue failed to regenerate convincingly), which again prevented a meaningful comparison across neighborhood sizes.
At this stage we revisited the experimental pipeline and concluded that the main problem was not simply ``insufficient compute'', but \textbf{confounding factors that made the neighborhood sweep hard to interpret when the models were already failing}.
In particular, when training is dominated by short teacher-forced transitions while evaluation is performed as free-running rollouts, low training loss does not necessarily translate into good long-horizon behavior.
In addition, mixture models can behave very differently depending on whether rule selection is evaluated with soft (differentiable) assignments or hard sampling; if this is not kept consistent, the observed differences can be dominated by sampling behavior rather than by NB.

\subsection{Step 3: controlled neighborhood sweep (final configuration)}

For these reasons, we temporarily stepped back from the long-horizon curriculum experiment and adopted a more controlled ablation aimed specifically at answering the neighborhood-size question.
We fixed the ``extra'' degrees of freedom that can dominate dynamics (most importantly, keeping the rule-selection temperature effectively constant during the sweep and using a consistent sampling mode at evaluation), and we generated an intermediate dataset that is large enough for stability while still tractable (\textbf{300 trajectories, 100 time steps}).

We then evaluated each trained model at multiple rollout horizons (e.g., \textbf{25, 50, 100 steps}) rather than relying on a single horizon.
This produces a clearer picture: even if different NB values look similar at very short horizons, improvements from larger neighborhoods are expected to show up as slower degradation and better stability as the rollout length increases.

In summary, curriculum learning was introduced as a pragmatic response to the computational cost of training on \textbf{1000$\times$500} simulations.
We then reverted to a simpler setting not because the curriculum is ``wrong'', but because we first needed an interpretable experimental protocol where the neighborhood-size effect is not masked by optimization and sampling confounds.
Once a robust NB trend is established under controlled conditions, curriculum learning can be reintroduced as a second-stage validation on selected neighborhoods to confirm that the effect persists in the full long-horizon regime.

\paragraph{Where the results are reported.}
Since this configuration is part of the experimental \emph{pipeline design} (and it directly motivates our final protocol), we report its detailed results in this Implementation chapter.
The \cref{c:results} chapter instead focuses on the overall thesis conclusions.

\paragraph{High-level outcome (brief).}
Under this controlled protocol, the neighborhood-size effect becomes observable.
In our sweep over \(NB \in \{1,\dots,7\}\), \(NB=1\) is consistently inadequate, while moving to \(NB\ge2\) yields a clear improvement.
Beyond this first jump, improvements are not strictly monotonic: different neighborhood sizes trade off distributional agreement and spatial fidelity, and differences become more evident as the rollout horizon increases (e.g., 25 vs 100 steps).

\subsubsection{Statistical tests (non-parametric)}
\label{sec:impl:step3:stats}
%----------------------------------------------------------

Finally, we add a lightweight statistical check to verify that the observed differences across neighborhood sizes are not explained only by noise.
This subsection explains what these tests are for, what they compute, and why we use non-parametric rather than parametric tests.

\paragraph{What they are for.}
The goal is to assess whether the differences in the six biological metrics across \(NB\in\{1,\dots,7\}\) are \emph{statistically significant}---i.e., unlikely to be due to random variation in the finite set of simulations.
Comparing against \(NB=1\) is not informative: \(NB=1\) is trivially inadequate (the model sees only the cell itself), so any \(NB\ge2\) would appear significantly better.
We therefore use two baselines, \(NB=2\) and \(NB=3\), and ask: ``Do we have enough evidence that larger neighborhood sizes perform differently from each baseline?'' This answers whether increasing the neighborhood beyond the minimal reasonable choice (\(NB=2\)) or beyond a common reference (\(NB=3\)) yields a measurable effect, and keeps conclusions robust to the choice of reference.

\paragraph{What they compute.}
We use two tests in sequence.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Kruskal--Wallis} (overall test): it ranks all observations (across all \(NB\)) for each metric, then tests whether the \emph{rank distributions} differ across the seven groups.
  It produces a \(p\)-value: if \(p\) is small (e.g.\ \(<0.05\)), we reject the null hypothesis that all \(NB\) groups have the same distribution of the metric; in other words, at least one neighborhood size behaves differently.
  It does \emph{not} assume normality; it only uses the ranks, so it is robust to outliers and heavy tails.
  \item \textbf{Mann--Whitney U} (post-hoc pairwise): once Kruskal--Wallis indicates that groups differ, we compare (separately) each \(NB>2\) to the baseline \(NB=2\), and each \(NB>3\) to the baseline \(NB=3\) (we do \emph{not} compare against \(NB=1\)).
  For each pair, the test ranks the pooled observations and computes a statistic from the sum of ranks of one group; the \(p\)-value tells us whether the two groups are drawn from the same distribution.
  We apply \textbf{Holm correction} within each baseline (five comparisons for vs.\ \(NB=2\), four for vs.\ \(NB=3\)) to control the family-wise error rate.
\end{itemize}
So, in short: Kruskal--Wallis tells us ``there is some difference among \(NB\)''; Mann--Whitney with Holm tells us ``which of the larger \(NB\) are significantly different from \(NB=2\) and from \(NB=3\)'' for each metric (see \cref{tab:impl:step3:stats}).

\paragraph{Why non-parametric.}
Parametric tests (e.g.\ one-way ANOVA and \(t\)-tests) assume that the metric values are drawn from a normal (Gaussian) distribution, or at least that the sample means are approximately normal by the central limit theorem.
In our setting, the per-simulation metrics (e.g.\ KL divergence, Chi-square) often have \emph{heavy-tailed} distributions: a few simulations can yield very large values, and the distributions are not obviously Gaussian.
Under these conditions, parametric tests can be anticonservative (too many false positives) or have unreliable \(p\)-values.
Non-parametric tests avoid distributional assumptions by working on \emph{ranks} instead of raw values: they only use the ordering of the data, so they are valid for any continuous distribution and are robust to outliers and skewness.
That is why we report Kruskal--Wallis and Mann--Whitney U rather than ANOVA and \(t\)-tests.

\paragraph{Interpretation and comparison of the two baselines.}
The Kruskal--Wallis \(p\)-values are below \(10^{-4}\) for all metrics and both models, so we have strong evidence that neighborhood size affects the distribution of each metric.
Comparing the two baseline columns reinforces and refines this conclusion.

\emph{Vs.\ \(NB=2\):} For the Mixture model, every metric lists \(NB=3,4,5,6,7\) as significant---i.e.\ even the first step beyond the minimum (\(NB=3\)) is distinguishable from \(NB=2\).
For the Stochastic model, the same holds for most metrics; exceptions are Border Size Diff (only \(4,5,6,7\): \(NB=3\) is not significantly different from \(NB=2\)) and, for Categorical MMD and Tumor Size Diff, \(NB=6\) is not in the significant set, so for those metrics \(NB=6\) is not clearly better than \(NB=2\).

\emph{Vs.\ \(NB=3\):} Here we only compare \(NB=4,5,6,7\) to \(NB=3\), so the column never contains 3.
For the Mixture model, Categorical MMD gives only \(4,5,6\) (so \(NB=7\) is not significantly different from \(NB=3\) for that metric); for all other metrics, \(4,5,6,7\) are significant vs.\ \(NB=3\).
For the Stochastic model, Spatial Variance Diff gives only \(4,5,7\) (so \(NB=6\) is not significantly different from \(NB=3\)), while Categorical MMD and Tumor Size Diff list \(4,5,6,7\) and Border Size Diff \(4,5,6,7\).

\emph{Takeaway:} The two baselines tell a consistent story: the effect of \(NB\) is statistically significant and not due to noise; going from \(NB=2\) to \(NB=3\) often (but not always, e.g.\ Stochastic Border Size) matters; beyond \(NB=3\), which of \(NB=4,5,6,7\) differ from the reference depends on the metric and the model.
So the ``best'' neighborhood size is metric- and model-dependent, and the tests with both baselines support the same overall message while making the dependence on the reference explicit.

%----------------------------------------------------------
\section{Curriculum learning experiment: setting and results}
\label{sec:impl:curriculum}
%----------------------------------------------------------

In the controlled sweep (Step 3) we saw that neighborhood size matters when we train on 300 trajectories of 100 steps and evaluate at 25, 50, and 100 steps.
We then asked: does the same hold when we train on longer trajectories (500 steps) using a curriculum?
This section describes that second experiment: what we did, how we evaluate the models, and what we see in the metrics and in the qualitative figures.
We also explain an important distinction---\emph{teacher forcing} during training vs.\ \emph{free rollouts} at evaluation---because it is the main reason why the model can look good on the training loss but still drift away from the ground truth when we run it for many steps.

\subsection{Why we ran this experiment}
\label{sec:impl:curriculum:rationale}

Step 3 used 300 trajectories of 100 steps and showed that \(NB\ge2\) helps and that the choice of \(NB\) matters at 25, 50, and 100 steps.
That experiment, however, does not tell us whether the same conclusions still hold when we train the model to match dynamics over 500 steps and then evaluate it at 25, 100, and 500 steps.
So we ran a separate experiment: 300 trajectories of 500 time steps each, with training split into three phases (short windows first, then longer ones).
The goal is to check that the neighborhood-size effect is not limited to the 100-step setting---that it carries over to the long-horizon regime we care about for tissue regeneration.
We also use the same six biological metrics as in Step 3, so we can compare the two experiments and see how the metrics change when we increase the evaluation step length (25, 100, or 500).

\subsection{Data and train/validation split}
\label{sec:impl:curriculum:data}

We use \textbf{300 simulated tissue-growth trajectories}, each of \textbf{500 time steps}, from the same complex tissue model as elsewhere in this thesis (see \cref{sec:impl:data}).
Each trajectory is a sequence of 2D grids of cell types (one-hot encoded for training).
We split the 300 histories into 80\% for training (240 trajectories) and 20\% for validation (60 trajectories), and we keep this split fixed.
Training is done only on the 240 training histories; when we report results, we run evaluation on all 300 (training and validation together) so that the comparison across neighborhood sizes and step lengths is fair and on the same set of initial conditions.
Before training we check that the loaded histories contain no NaN or Inf, to avoid numerical issues.

\subsection{Curriculum schedule and training protocol}
\label{sec:impl:curriculum:schedule}

We train in \textbf{three phases}, each with a longer time window.
The idea is to start with short windows (cheap to train) and then move to longer ones, so the model first learns short-term dynamics and then refines them on 500-step windows without paying the full cost of long backpropagation from the very beginning.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Phase 1:} windows of 35 steps, 250 epochs, learning rate \(0.0005\), update every 1 step. The loss is computed at every step inside the window.
\item \textbf{Phase 2:} windows of 100 steps, 150 epochs, learning rate \(0.0005\), update every 1 step.
\item \textbf{Phase 3:} windows of 500 steps, 90 epochs, learning rate \(0.0001\), update every 2 steps.
\end{itemize}

In each phase we use early stopping on the training loss (we stop if there is no improvement for 20 epochs and then restore the best weights of that phase).
The loss is mean squared error on the one-hot state; we compute it at every step in the window, not only at the last step, so the model gets a learning signal from all intermediate states.
We train only the \textbf{Mixture NCA} and the \textbf{Stochastic Mixture NCA}; we skip the Standard NCA and the NCA with noise.
For each neighborhood size we save a checkpoint at the end of each phase and a final checkpoint that we use for all evaluation below.

We do \emph{not} compute a validation loss during training: the 60 validation histories are never used in the training loop.
We only use the training loss for early stopping.
So we cannot see a train--validation gap to detect overfitting; we keep the pipeline simple and avoid an extra pass over the validation set at every epoch.
After training we judge the model by running the evaluation protocol (free rollouts at 25, 100, and 500 steps on all 300 histories and computing the six biological metrics); those metrics are not used to choose or stop the model, only to report and compare.

\subsection{Teacher forcing vs.\ free rollout: what changes and why it matters}
\label{sec:impl:curriculum:teacher_vs_free}

During training we use \emph{teacher forcing}.
That means: when we compute the loss for a window of, say, 35 steps, we feed the model the \emph{true} state from the dataset at each step.
Step 0: we give the model the true initial state \(x_0\), it predicts \(\hat{x}_1\).
Step 1: we give the model the \emph{true} \(x_1\) (not \(\hat{x}_1\)), it predicts \(\hat{x}_2\).
And so on.
So at every step the model sees the correct previous state; its errors are not fed back.
The loss is the mismatch between its predictions and the true next states, and we backpropagate through the whole window.
This is efficient and gives a strong learning signal, but the model never practices recovering from its own mistakes.

During evaluation we use \emph{free rollouts} (no teacher forcing).
We take an initial state \(x_0\) from the dataset and we run the model for 25, 100, or 500 steps.
Step 0: we give \(x_0\), the model outputs \(\hat{x}_1\).
Step 1: we give \emph{\(\hat{x}_1\)} (the model's own prediction), it outputs \(\hat{x}_2\).
Step 2: we give \(\hat{x}_2\), and so on.
So the model never sees the true state again after \(x_0\); it only sees its own predictions.
If it makes a small error at step 10, that error is the input for step 11, and the error can grow over time.
After hundreds of steps, the generated tissue can look quite different from the ground truth even if the training loss was low.

The difference between the two regimes is intentional.
At evaluation we want to answer: given only an initial condition, as in real use, can the model produce a good trajectory?
We do not have access to the true states along the way, so the only meaningful test is to run the model on its own; that is exactly what a free rollout does.
Using teacher forcing at evaluation would mean feeding the model the ground truth at every step, which would not reflect any realistic scenario and would make the metrics overly optimistic.

During training, we could in principle also use free rollouts: unroll the model for many steps, feed it its own predictions, and backpropagate through the whole trajectory.
That would train the model in the same regime as evaluation and could reduce the gap.
In practice, however, training with long free rollouts is more expensive and less stable: gradients must flow through hundreds of steps, and optimization becomes harder.
Teacher forcing avoids that by giving a clean, step-by-step signal and is the standard choice for sequence and trajectory models.
The downside is that the model never learns to correct its own errors; this train--eval mismatch is often called \emph{exposure bias}.
A possible compromise is \emph{scheduled teacher forcing}: start with full teacher forcing and gradually feed the true state less often during training, so the model learns to cope with its own predictions.
We do not use that here; we keep the pipeline simple and accept that part of the gap at long times comes from this design choice.
When we report metrics and show qualitative figures, we always use free rollouts, so the numbers and images reflect the real use case.

\subsection{Models and evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:impl:curriculum:models}

The model architectures (Mixture NCA and Stochastic Mixture NCA) are introduced in \cref{sec:sota:mnca}.
Here we use the same setup as elsewhere in the thesis: extended neighborhood, 6 state channels (one per cell type), 5 mixture rules, hidden size 128.
We train and evaluate only for \(NB \in \{2, 3, 5\}\) to keep the experiment manageable while still covering a range of neighborhood sizes.

At evaluation we run \textbf{free rollouts} (as just described): from each initial state we unroll the model for 25, 100, or 500 steps with no teacher forcing, and we compute the six biological metrics (KL divergence, Chi-square, categorical MMD, tumor size difference, border size difference, spatial variance difference) by comparing the \emph{final} generated state to the ground-truth state at the same step.
For the Stochastic Mixture NCA we use hard rule sampling (multinomial sampling at each step), and we run 10 rollouts per initial condition (different random seeds) and report the mean and standard deviation of the metrics.
We use the same sampling and temperature choices as in Step 3 so that the results are comparable.

\paragraph{Ground truth.}
The simulator produces robust, organic growth: small clusters at \(t=10\) expand into intricate, irregular or branching structures by \(t=500\), with clear boundaries between cell types and controlled, localized growth that does not fill the grid.

\paragraph{Mixture NCA (deterministic).}

\paragraph{Stochastic Mixture NCA.}
At \(t=10\) the stochastic variant also tracks the ground truth well.
Over time it performs \emph{noticeably better} than the deterministic Mixture NCA: it keeps the overall scale and footprint of the patterns closer to the ground truth, avoids the runaway expansion, and preserves more organic shapes and irregular boundaries.
For \(NB=3\) and \(NB=5\) (Figures~\ref{fig:impl:curriculum:ex_nb3}--\ref{fig:impl:curriculum:ex_nb5}), the qualitative agreement with the ground truth at \(t=50\), \(t=100\), and \(t=500\) is strong---growth remains controlled and morphologies are close to the target.

In summary, the stochastic component is crucial for stable, morphologically plausible long-horizon behavior and aligns with the better distributional metrics we report for the stochastic model at larger \(NB\) and longer step lengths.

\subsection{Metric results and interpretation}
\label{sec:impl:curriculum:metrics}

After training we run the evaluation protocol (free rollouts at 25, 100, and 500 steps) for each combination of neighborhood size and step length, and we record the six biological metrics.
Figure~\ref{fig:impl:curriculum:dashboard} reports the \emph{mean} of each metric over the three step lengths (25, 100, 500) for each neighborhood size and each model; Table~\ref{tab:impl:curriculum:metrics_summary} summarizes which configuration is best per metric and explains the contrast between metric types.

\paragraph{Distributional metrics (KL divergence, Chi-square, categorical MMD).}
These metrics measure how closely the \emph{global statistical distribution} of cell states (or derived counts) in the generated pattern matches the ground truth---e.g., proportions of cell types, overall composition.
On all three, \textbf{Stochastic Mixture NCA} clearly outperforms Mixture NCA, and the gap widens as \(NB\) increases: at \(NB=5\) the stochastic model attains the lowest values (e.g., KL and Chi-square roughly halved or better vs.\ the deterministic one; categorical MMD much lower at \(NB=5\)).
The deterministic Mixture NCA improves from \(NB=2\) to \(NB=3\) but then plateaus or slightly degrades (e.g., Chi-square and categorical MMD rise again from \(NB=3\) to \(NB=5\)), as if the larger receptive field does not help it further for distributional fidelity.
The stochastic variant, by contrast, benefits consistently from a larger neighborhood: it can explore the state space more and, with more context, better approximate the target distribution.

\paragraph{Spatial metrics (tumor size diff, border size diff, spatial variance diff).}
These metrics measure \emph{geometric and structural} accuracy: size of the active region, extent of boundaries, and spatial clustering or dispersion.
Here the picture is more nuanced and the two models behave differently.
\textbf{Mixture NCA} often performs best at \textbf{\(NB=3\)} for border size and spatial variance: the deterministic rule, with a moderate neighborhood, can form stable local structures and sharp boundaries without the extra variability that stochasticity introduces.
\textbf{Stochastic Mixture NCA} exhibits a pronounced \emph{peak in error at \(NB=3\)} on border size and spatial variance (worse than at \(NB=2\) or \(NB=5\)), suggesting that at this scale the randomness interferes with precise spatial coherence.
At \textbf{\(NB=5\)}, the stochastic model recovers: it improves on tumor size diff, border size diff, and spatial variance diff, and at \(NB=5\) often matches or beats the deterministic model on these spatial metrics, while the deterministic one can degrade (e.g., spatial variance diff rises from \(NB=3\) to \(NB=5\)).
At \(NB=2\), Mixture NCA sometimes has an edge on spatial metrics (e.g., tumor size diff), but both models are less stable at long horizons when \(NB\) is small.

\paragraph{Why distributional and spatial metrics give different results.}
Distributional metrics aggregate over the whole pattern and reward a good match of \emph{overall} counts and proportions; they benefit from the stochastic model's ability to explore and from a larger receptive field (\(NB=5\)), which provides more context for consistent long-horizon behavior.
Spatial metrics, in contrast, are sensitive to \emph{local} geometry: sharp borders, coherent regions, and stable spatial arrangement.
Stochasticity can help globally but add local ``noise'' or blurring, which hurts border and spatial-variance accuracy at an intermediate scale (\(NB=3\)); the deterministic model, with no sampling noise, can excel at that scale for structure.
When \(NB\) is increased to 5, the stochastic model gains enough context to stabilize its spatial behavior (or the randomness averages out better), so it recovers and often surpasses the deterministic one even on spatial metrics.
Hence there is no single ``best'' \(NB\) or model for all metrics: the choice depends on whether one prioritizes distributional agreement (favor Stochastic Mixture NCA and \(NB=5\)) or fine-grained spatial fidelity at a given scale (where \(NB=3\) can be best for the deterministic model, and \(NB=5\) for the stochastic one).

\paragraph{How the metrics change with step length.}
We evaluate at 25, 100, and 500 steps to see how performance changes as the rollout gets longer.
At \textbf{25 steps} we are close to what the model saw in the first curriculum phase (35-step windows), so errors stay low and the difference between \(NB=2\), \(NB=3\), and \(NB=5\) is small.
At \textbf{100} and \textbf{500 steps} the metrics generally worsen and the receptive field matters more: distributional metrics are clearly best for \(NB=5\), especially for the stochastic model; spatial metrics show the trade-offs described above (e.g., Mixture NCA best at \(NB=3\) for some, Stochastic best at \(NB=5\) after recovering from the \(NB=3\) peak).
Figure~\ref{fig:impl:curriculum:categorical_mmd} breaks out categorical MMD by step length; the dashboard (Figure~\ref{fig:impl:curriculum:dashboard}) averages over step lengths and gives the overall picture.

\paragraph{Comparison with Step 3.}
In Step 3 we had 300 trajectories of 100 steps, no curriculum, and we evaluated at 25, 50, and 100 steps with \(NB\) from 1 to 7.
Here we have 300 trajectories of 500 steps, a three-phase curriculum (35, 100, 500), and we evaluate at 25, 100, and 500 steps with \(NB \in \{2,3,5\}\).
The conclusions match: in both experiments, larger neighborhoods help on distributional metrics at longer horizons, and no single \(NB\) is best on every metric.
Step 3 showed that \(NB=1\) is too small and that \(NB\ge2\) gives a clear improvement; this curriculum run shows that among 2, 3, and 5, the larger one (\(NB=5\)) is better at 100 and 500 steps, especially for the stochastic model.
We use the same six metrics in both experiments, so the comparison is fair; the curriculum experiment simply pushes the evaluation horizon to 500 steps and confirms that the neighborhood-size effect is still there.

\subsection{Why generated images differ from ground truth and possible improvements}
\label{sec:impl:curriculum:limitations}

When we look at the qualitative figures (Figures~\ref{fig:impl:curriculum:ex_nb2}--\ref{fig:impl:curriculum:ex_nb5}), we see that the stochastic variant stays close to the ground truth for \(NB=3\) and \(NB=5\) at all time points, with controlled growth and organic shapes; at \(NB=2\) it still keeps the right scale but the internal structure can look noisier.
The deterministic Mixture NCA, on the other hand, matches the ground truth only early on (around \(t=10\)). As time goes on it starts to diverge: the pattern expands in an uncontrolled way, becomes blocky and homogeneous, and by \(t=500\) the generated tissue often has a different layout, a different mix of cell types, and a tendency to fill the grid instead of evolving like the simulator.
It is natural to ask why this happens and what could be done about it.

Several factors contribute.
During training we use teacher forcing: at every step we feed the model the true state from the dataset, so small errors do not accumulate and the loss can stay low.
At evaluation we do the opposite: we run free rollouts, where the model only sees its own previous output. In that regime, a small mistake at step 10 is fed into step 11, and the error can grow over time. By step 500 the trajectory may have drifted far from the ground truth even if the model had learned the short-term dynamics well.
A second point is what we actually optimize: the loss is a step-by-step mean squared error over the training window, which encourages correct one-step predictions but does not directly reward how well a long unrolled trajectory matches a single ground-truth run.
For the stochastic model there is an extra nuance: at each step we sample a rule, so different random seeds produce different trajectories; the ground truth we compare against is just one possible realization of the simulator, and part of the ``difference'' we see is therefore normal variability between runs, not only a failure of the model.
Finally, the last curriculum phase uses 500-step windows but with fewer epochs and a smaller learning rate, so we may stop before the model has fully learned to stay on track for the full 500 steps.

Several directions could be explored to reduce the gap at long times (see also \cref{sec:conc:future}): training longer on 500-step windows or adding more curriculum phases; computing a validation loss with free rollouts and using it for early stopping; adding losses that target the final state of a rollout; or using scheduled teacher forcing so that during training the model gradually sees the true state less often. At evaluation, running multiple rollouts and averaging or selecting among them could improve the metrics. We leave these ideas for future work. The current experiment already shows that the neighborhood-size effect carries over to the long-horizon curriculum setting and that a larger \(NB\) helps at 100 and 500 steps, especially for the stochastic model.

In summary, the curriculum run confirms what we saw in Step 3: when we train on 500-step trajectories with a phased curriculum, a larger neighborhood still helps, in particular at long evaluation horizons and for the stochastic variant, while the deterministic one exhibits a more mixed trade-off across metrics and horizons. A short takeaway is in \cref{sec:results:takeaway}.

\section{BBBC031 microscopy segmentation experiment}
\label{sec:impl:bbbc031}
%----------------------------------------------------------

To complement the synthetic tissue experiments, we ran a separate experiment on a real imaging dataset: BBBC031, a public benchmark for cell segmentation in fluorescence microscopy.
This section describes the dataset, the motivation for the experiment, the setup and results obtained so far, their interpretation, and the planned re-training for the larger neighborhood size with a view to reporting updated results once that training is complete.

\subsection{The BBBC031 dataset}
\label{sec:impl:bbbc031:dataset}

BBBC031 is a publicly available dataset from the Broad Bioimage Benchmark Collection~\cite{bbbc031_ref} (BBBC).
It consists of fluorescence microscopy images of human U2OS cells, with ground-truth annotations that include cell centroids and, for a subset of images, full cell masks.
The mask images are provided in RGBA format: the alpha channel (or the composite of RGB and alpha) indicates the segmented cell regions.
We use these cell-mask images as the \emph{target} for the NCA: the model is trained to grow a segmentation from a small set of seed points (the annotated centroids) until it matches the ground-truth mask.

This task is conceptually similar to the synthetic tissue experiments---the model must learn a local update rule that, when iterated from an initial state (seeds on a grid), produces a desired spatial pattern---but the target is a real microscopy image rather than a simulated history.
It therefore serves as a sanity check and a bridge toward real-world imaging applications: if the same Stochastic Mixture NCA framework can learn to segment cells from seeds on BBBC031, it supports the generality of the approach; at the same time, the fixed image resolution (we resize to \(96\times96\)) and the small number of images used keep the experiment manageable and reproducible (e.g., on Google Colab).

\subsection{Motivation for the experiment}
\label{sec:impl:bbbc031:motivation}

The main thesis experiments use a synthetic tissue simulator and focus on the effect of neighborhood size (\(NB\)) and curriculum design on long-horizon behavior.
We wanted to test whether the same MNCA architecture and training pipeline could be applied in a different setting: \emph{image-based segmentation} from seed points, with a single static target image per training run (rather than a time series of grids).
The goals were:
\begin{itemize}
  \item to verify that the extended Stochastic Mixture NCA (with configurable neighborhood size) can be trained on real microscopy data;
  \item to compare two neighborhood sizes, \(NB=3\) and \(NB=7\), under the same optimization schedule, and to observe whether the loss and segmentation quality behave similarly to what we saw in the synthetic experiments (where \(NB\ge2\) helped and very small \(NB\) was insufficient);
  \item to produce quantitative metrics (IoU, Dice, MSE on the predicted vs.\ ground-truth mask) suitable for the thesis and for comparison with the synthetic results.
\end{itemize}

The training protocol mirrors the one used in the reference MNCA microscopy setup: we train on one image at a time (one cell-mask target per run), with a pool of states and random rollout lengths between 10 and 20 steps per optimization step; we run 4000 optimization steps (gradient updates) with Adam and a multi-step learning-rate schedule.
At inference we run the model for a fixed number of \emph{simulation} steps (20) from the seed state to obtain the final predicted mask; this is the number that appears in the comparison figures (e.g., ``MNCA after 20 steps'').
It is important to distinguish \textbf{4000 training steps} (number of weight updates) from \textbf{20 simulation steps} (number of NCA updates used to generate the output image): the former is the length of optimization; the latter is the length of the rollout at evaluation.

\subsection{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:impl:bbbc031:setup}

We selected five images from BBBC031 that have corresponding cell-mask annotations (same ordering as in the Colab notebook: the first five such images in the ground-truth annotations).
For each image we trained two models: one with neighborhood size \(NB=3\) and one with \(NB=7\), using the Stochastic Mixture NCA (Extended Mixture NCA with noise), with the same hyperparameters (learning rate \(10^{-3}\), milestones at 1500 and 3000 steps, target size \(96\times96\), 24 state channels, 5 rules, hidden dimension 128).
Each run produces a saved checkpoint and a loss history (one scalar per optimization step).
After training, we run inference with 20 simulation steps, compare the predicted RGBA output to the ground-truth mask, and compute IoU (intersection over union) and Dice coefficient on the binarized alpha channel (threshold 0.5), and MSE on the full RGBA channels (same as the training loss).
The metrics are aggregated in a table for the thesis (\cref{tab:bbbc031_metrics}).

\subsection{Results obtained}
\label{sec:impl:bbbc031:results}

Training for 4000 steps with the default learning rate yielded a clear asymmetry between the two neighborhood sizes.

\paragraph{Loss curves.}
For \textbf{\(NB=3\)}, all five images converged to low loss: the final loss values are on the order of \(10^{-4}\)--\(10^{-3}\), and the mean loss over the last 200 steps is similarly low.
For \textbf{\(NB=7\)}, two images (img0 and img1) exhibited \emph{divergent} training: the loss rose to values on the order of \(10^3\) and did not recover.
The other three images (img2--img4) reached much lower loss than img0/img1 but still an order of magnitude or more above the typical \(NB=3\) loss (final loss roughly in the range 10--80 vs.\ \(10^{-4}\)--\(10^{-3}\) for \(NB=3\)).
A trend analysis over the last 500 steps shows that the loss for \(NB=7\) was still slightly decreasing (negative slope) in all cases, but for the diverged runs the absolute level is so high that simply extending the same training would not be practical; for the non-diverged runs the loss might decrease further with more steps or a lower learning rate.

\subsection{Interpretation}
\label{sec:impl:bbbc031:interpretation}

The results are consistent with the hypothesis that, under the \emph{same} optimization schedule (learning rate, number of steps, pool size, etc.), the larger neighborhood \(NB=7\) is harder to train than \(NB=3\) on this task.
For \(NB=3\), the model has fewer parameters in the perception pathway and a smaller receptive field; optimization is stable and reaches a good minimum.
For \(NB=7\), the perception kernels are larger and the dynamics may be more sensitive to the learning rate; with the default \(10^{-3}\), two of the five runs diverged and the other three converged to a worse minimum than \(NB=3\).

This does not imply that \(NB=7\) is inherently unsuitable for BBBC031.
Rather, it suggests that training with \(NB=7\) requires different hyperparameters---in particular a \emph{lower learning rate} and possibly a longer or differently scheduled optimization---so that the model can converge to a competitive solution instead of diverging or stalling at high loss.
In the synthetic experiments we observed that neighborhood size has a measurable effect on long-horizon behavior and that larger \(NB\) can improve distributional and sometimes spatial metrics when training is stable; the BBBC031 experiment extends that comparison to a real imaging dataset and shows that stability of training itself can depend on \(NB\) when other hyperparameters are held fixed.

\subsection{Re-training for \(NB=7\): parameters and motivation}
\label{sec:impl:bbbc031:retrain}

Given the current results, continuing the existing \(NB=7\) runs for more steps with the same learning rate is unlikely to help: for the diverged runs (img0, img1) the loss is already in the thousands, and for the others the gain from a few extra thousand steps would be limited.
We therefore \textbf{re-train the \(NB=7\) models} from scratch with different hyperparameters, chosen so that optimization remains stable while still allowing convergence to a good minimum.

\paragraph{Parameters used for NB=7 re-training.}
For \(NB=7\) we use:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Learning rate:} \(3\times10^{-4}\) (instead of \(10^{-3}\) used for \(NB=3\)).
  \item \textbf{Optimization steps:} 6000 (instead of 4000).
  \item \textbf{LR schedule milestones:} 2500 and 4500 (so the learning rate is reduced at roughly 1/3 and 2/3 of the run, preserving the same relative schedule as for \(NB=3\) with milestones at 1500 and 3000).
\end{itemize}
All other hyperparameters (batch size, pool size, min/max simulation steps per episode, target size, architecture) are unchanged and match the \(NB=3\) runs.
The Colab notebook that runs the training is updated so that it applies these settings automatically when \(nb=7\); for \(nb=3\) it keeps the original defaults (learning rate \(10^{-3}\), 4000 steps, milestones 1500 and 3000).

\paragraph{Motivation for these choices.}
With the default learning rate \(10^{-3}\), the \(NB=7\) model has a larger and more sensitive parameterization (larger perception kernels and more weights in the extended neighborhood pathway), and the loss either diverged (img0, img1) or converged to a poor minimum (img2--img4).
\emph{Reducing the learning rate} to \(3\times10^{-4}\) is a standard way to stabilize training when the same schedule leads to divergence or high loss: smaller updates limit overshooting and allow the optimizer to settle into a better basin.
We do not go as low as \(10^{-4}\) in the first re-run so that training remains reasonably fast while still being stable; if needed, a further run with \(10^{-4}\) can be tried.
\emph{Increasing the number of steps} to 6000 compensates for the smaller learning rate: each update is smaller, so more updates are needed to reach a comparable reduction in loss; 6000 steps with LR \(3\times10^{-4}\) give the optimizer more time to converge without changing the per-step cost.
\emph{Adjusting the milestones} to 2500 and 4500 keeps the same relative schedule (two decay points at about 1/3 and 2/3 of the run), so the learning rate drops in the second half of training in a way that is consistent with the \(NB=3\) setup.
Together, these choices are intended to yield stable, convergent training for \(NB=7\) on all five images, so that we can fairly compare segmentation quality (IoU, Dice, MSE) and qualitative appearance between \(NB=3\) and \(NB=7\) under conditions where both neighborhood sizes have converged.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:impl:threats}
%----------------------------------------------------------

The conclusions of this chapter are subject to several limitations that are worth stating explicitly.
First, all experiments rely on a \emph{synthetic} tissue simulator with fixed parameters (cell types, differentiation rules, grid size); the effect of neighborhood size may depend on these choices and may not transfer unchanged to real biological data or to other toy models.
Second, the datasets used are of moderate size (300 trajectories of 100 or 500 steps); larger or more diverse datasets could alter the relative performance of different \(NB\) values.
Third, the neighborhood size is varied only in the range \(NB \in \{1,\dots,7\}\) (or \(\{2,3,5\}\) in the curriculum experiment); we do not claim that the observed trade-offs extend to much larger receptive fields.
Fourth, in the curriculum experiment we do not compute a validation loss or use free-rollout-based early stopping, so we cannot quantify overfitting; the reported metrics are purely for comparison across configurations, not for model selection.
Acknowledging these limitations helps interpret the results and suggests directions for more generalizable follow-up work (see \cref{sec:conc:future}).
