This chapter describes the implementation and experimental pipeline of the thesis.
The first part covers data generation (the biological toy model and the notion of histories) and how NCA-style models are trained and evaluated.
The central part is devoted to the \emph{controlled neighbourhood sweep} (Step~3): a protocol designed to isolate the effect of spatial neighbourhood size \(NB\) on long-horizon behaviour, including the evaluation metrics and the statistical testing methodology used to compare neighbourhoods.
The final part presents a second experiment, \emph{curriculum learning} on 500-step trajectories, describing the curriculum schedule and the train--evaluation distinction (teacher forcing vs.\ free rollout) that shapes long-horizon behaviour.
All empirical results, figures, and comparisons are reported in \cref{c:results}.

%----------------------------------------------------------
\section{How experiments are structured}
%----------------------------------------------------------

The experimental work in this thesis is organized as a sequence of increasingly controlled studies.
The main research question is whether increasing the \emph{spatial neighbourhood size} (\(NB\)) in Mixture Neural Cellular Automata improves long-horizon behaviour in a tissue-growth setting.
In practice, the experiments are implemented as training/evaluation pipelines that can be repeated across multiple neighbourhood sizes, while keeping all other hyperparameters fixed whenever possible.

At a high level, each experiment follows the same steps:
\begin{itemize}
\item generate or load a dataset of tissue-growth trajectories (``histories'');
\item train one or more NCA variants on short temporal windows extracted from these histories;
\item evaluate the trained models by running free rollouts (no teacher forcing) for different horizons;
\item compute quantitative metrics and inspect qualitative rollouts.
\end{itemize}

%----------------------------------------------------------
\section{Data generation and biological toy model}
\label{sec:impl:data}
%----------------------------------------------------------

Before training NCAs, we need supervised trajectories representing plausible tissue dynamics.
In this thesis, these trajectories are generated by a simple stochastic, biologically-inspired simulator and collected through a small data-generation pipeline.

\paragraph{State representation (cell types).}
Each simulation state is a 2D grid of discrete cell types.
In the ``complex'' configuration used for most experiments, the simulator includes a small differentiation hierarchy (e.g., \texttt{STEM}, \texttt{INTERMEDIATE}, and \texttt{DIFFERENTIATED} types), plus an \texttt{EMPTY} background.

\paragraph{Local update rules (biological intuition).}
At each simulation step, each occupied cell can:
\begin{itemize}
  \item die,
  \item survive, or
  \item divide into a nearby empty site.
\end{itemize}
When a division happens, the newly created cell may \emph{differentiate} according to a probability table that depends on the parent cell type and (optionally) on neighbor context.
This yields trajectories that show growth, spatial organization, and gradual maturation into differentiated compartments.

\paragraph{Histories.}
For a single simulation we record the entire time series of grids \(x_0, x_1, \dots, x_T\).
We refer to this time series as a \emph{history}.
In the thesis we may visualize a history either as a sequence of snapshots (selected time points) or as a small montage, because it helps identify qualitative behaviors (stable growth, collapse, frozen patterns, etc.).

\paragraph{Dataset definition.}
Formally, the dataset is a collection of histories:
\[
\mathcal{D}=\{x^{(i)}_{0:T}\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]
where each \(x^{(i)}_t\) is a discrete 2D grid state at time \(t\).
In this work we use multiple datasets with different \(N\) and \(T\) depending on the experiment.
Throughout the thesis we use the term ``ground truth'' to refer to trajectories and final states generated by this simulator (for synthetic experiments) or to the annotated microscopy masks in BBBC031 (for the segmentation task); all models are trained and evaluated by comparing their outputs against these reference data.

\paragraph{How a single history is generated (example).}
To generate one history, we start from an initial condition where a small cluster of stem-like cells is placed near the center of the grid (with small randomness), while all other sites are empty.
Then, for each time step, cells update locally:
each occupied site may die, survive, or divide into a nearby empty site; if a division occurs, the newly created cell may differentiate according to a type-dependent probability table that can also be influenced by the local neighbourhood composition.

The goal is not to perfectly match biology, but to produce trajectories with growth and spatial organization that can serve as supervised targets for learning a local update rule.

\section{From trajectories to update rules}
%----------------------------------------------------------

All NCA-style models in this thesis learn a \emph{local} update rule that is applied repeatedly over time.
Conceptually, a standard formulation is:
\begin{equation}
x_{t+1} = x_t + f_{\theta}\!\left(\mathcal{P}(x_t)\right),
\end{equation}
where \(x_t\) is the grid state at time \(t\), \(\mathcal{P}(\cdot)\) is a local perception operator (it extracts neighbourhood information), and \(f_{\theta}\) is a neural network that predicts an update.
Mixture models extend this idea by having multiple candidate update rules and a learned mechanism to select (or mix) them over space and time \cite{mnca_milite2025}.

In training, the models are usually optimised on short temporal windows extracted from longer histories (teacher-forced supervision), while evaluation is performed as free-running rollouts.
This gap is important when we compare neighbourhood sizes, because a low short-horizon loss does not automatically imply stable long-horizon behaviour.

\paragraph{Example of generation by the learned model (multi-step rollout).}
After training, we can use a model to generate a trajectory by starting from an initial grid \(x_0\) and iterating the learned update rule for multiple steps, producing \(\hat{x}_1, \hat{x}_2, \dots, \hat{x}_T\).
Figure~\ref{fig:impl:model:rollout_example} shows an example rollout over multiple steps (same initial condition as \cref{fig:impl:data:history_example}), illustrating what it means for the model to ``free-run''.

\section{Reference experimental setting (MNCA paper)}
%----------------------------------------------------------

The starting point for our experiments is the training protocol used in the reference MNCA paper \cite{mnca_milite2025}.
In its ``paper-like'' regime, training uses a relatively small dataset and a short temporal horizon, with a long optimisation schedule.
We replicate this setting as closely as possible and then change only the neighbourhood size \(NB\), to isolate its effect.

%----------------------------------------------------------
\section{Experiment: Impact of Neighborhood Extension}
%----------------------------------------------------------

Our first experiment had a simple goal: understand whether extending the spatial neighbourhood in MNCA is a meaningful direction.
We initially followed the paper-like regime, and then progressively changed the data and training schedule to strengthen supervision and make long-horizon training feasible.
Overall, the experiment was carried out in three steps (three configurations).

\subsection{Summary of steps 1 and 2}

We started from the training setup used in the reference paper: a relatively small dataset (\textbf{200 simulated trajectories}) with a short temporal horizon (\textbf{35 time steps}) trained for \textbf{800 epochs}.
Our first goal was not to change the learning procedure itself, but to isolate the effect of the \emph{spatial neighbourhood size} (NB) by repeating the same training protocol across multiple neighbourhoods.
In principle, increasing NB should allow the update rule to condition on a broader spatial context, which could improve regeneration and pattern stability.

In practice, this ``paper-like'' regime did not produce a clean answer to the neighbourhood question.
Across several NB values, the qualitative rollouts remained inconsistent and the quantitative differences were small, making it hard to tell whether:
\begin{itemize}
  \item NB genuinely had little impact, or
  \item the training signal was simply too weak/noisy in this low-data, short-horizon setting.
\end{itemize}
At that point, we were not confident that a negative result would be meaningful.

Following this, we attempted to strengthen the supervision by increasing both dataset size and sequence length, moving to \textbf{1000 trajectories of 500 time steps} each.
The motivation was twofold:
\begin{itemize}
  \item more trajectories reduce variance and improve coverage of initial conditions;
  \item longer sequences better represent the long-horizon behaviour we ultimately care about when judging whether a model ``regenerates'' rather than just matching short-term transitions.
\end{itemize}
However, training directly on long sequences at this scale was computationally heavy and slowed iteration substantially.

To make long-horizon training feasible, we introduced \textbf{curriculum learning}~\cite{bengio2009curriculum}, training on progressively longer temporal windows (e.g., a first phase with a \textbf{35-step} window, then \textbf{100-step}, and finally \textbf{500-step}, with fewer epochs and smaller learning rates in later phases).
The intended effect of the curriculum was to let the models first learn stable short-term local dynamics and then refine them under longer-horizon constraints without paying the full cost of long unrolls from the beginning.

Despite the curriculum, the resulting rollouts were often poor (the tissue failed to regenerate convincingly), which again prevented a meaningful comparison across neighbourhood sizes.
At this stage we revisited the experimental pipeline and concluded that the main problem was not simply ``insufficient compute'', but \textbf{confounding factors that made the neighbourhood sweep hard to interpret when the models were already failing}.
In particular, when training is dominated by short teacher-forced transitions while evaluation is performed as free-running rollouts, low training loss does not necessarily translate into good long-horizon behaviour.
In addition, mixture models can behave very differently depending on whether rule selection is evaluated with soft (differentiable) assignments or hard sampling; if this is not kept consistent, the observed differences can be dominated by sampling behaviour rather than by NB.

\subsection{Step 3: controlled neighbourhood sweep (final configuration)}

For these reasons, we temporarily stepped back from the long-horizon curriculum experiment and adopted a more controlled ablation aimed specifically at answering the neighbourhood-size question.
We fixed the main degrees of freedom that can dominate dynamics (most importantly, keeping the rule-selection temperature effectively constant during the sweep and using a consistent sampling mode at evaluation), and we generated an intermediate dataset that is large enough for stability while still tractable (300 trajectories, 100 time steps).
Each trained model is then evaluated at multiple rollout horizons (e.g., 25, 50, 100 steps) rather than relying on a single horizon, so that neighbourhood effects can emerge as the rollout length increases.

This configuration is part of the experimental \emph{pipeline design} and directly motivates the final protocol; the corresponding numerical results and figures are reported in \cref{sec:results:neighborhood}.

\subsubsection{Step 3: evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:impl:step3:results}
%----------------------------------------------------------

We evaluate models trained with neighbourhood sizes \(NB \in \{1,\dots,7\}\) using both \emph{distributional} and \emph{spatial} metrics computed on the final state of each generated simulation.
The reference dataset consists of 300 ground-truth histories with a horizon of 100 steps (stored as a serialized array for reproducibility).
For each neighbourhood size, models are evaluated at multiple generation horizons (Step Length \(=25,50,100\)).
For each configuration we report aggregated performance (mean and variability across repeated evaluations) and we additionally inspect \emph{per-simulation} distributions to assess robustness and failure modes (heavy tails and outliers).
Detailed numerical trends, figures, and comparisons for this sweep are presented in \cref{sec:results:neighborhood}.

In addition to scalar metrics, we use qualitative diagnostics to interpret how different neighbourhood sizes operate.
In particular, we visualize \emph{rule-assignment probability maps} \(p(r \mid x)\) for selected example states to inspect spatial specialization patterns; these visualizations are reported in the Results chapter together with the other Step~3 analyses.

\subsubsection{Statistical tests (non-parametric)}
\label{sec:impl:step3:stats}
%----------------------------------------------------------

Finally, we add a lightweight statistical check to verify that the observed differences across neighbourhood sizes are not explained only by noise.
This subsection explains what these tests are for, what they compute, and why we use non-parametric rather than parametric tests.

\textit{What they are for.}
The goal is to assess whether the differences in the six biological metrics across \(NB\in\{1,\dots,7\}\) are \emph{statistically significant}, i.e.\ unlikely to be due to random variation in the finite set of simulations.
Comparing against \(NB=1\) is not informative: \(NB=1\) is trivially inadequate (the model sees only the cell itself), so any \(NB\ge2\) would appear significantly better.
We therefore use two baselines, \(NB=2\) and \(NB=3\), and ask: ``Do we have enough evidence that larger neighbourhood sizes perform differently from each baseline?'' This answers whether increasing the neighbourhood beyond the minimal reasonable choice (\(NB=2\)) or beyond a common reference (\(NB=3\)) yields a measurable effect, and keeps conclusions robust to the choice of reference.

\textit{What they compute.}
We use two tests in sequence.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Kruskal-Wallis} (overall test): it ranks all observations (across all \(NB\)) for each metric, then tests whether the \emph{rank distributions} differ across the seven groups.
  It produces a \(p\)-value: if \(p\) is small (e.g.\ \(<0.05\)), we reject the null hypothesis that all \(NB\) groups have the same distribution of the metric; in other words, at least one neighbourhood size behaves differently.
  It does \emph{not} assume normality; it only uses the ranks, so it is robust to outliers and heavy tails~\cite{gibbons_chakraborti2020}.
  \item \textbf{Mann-Whitney U} (post-hoc pairwise): once Kruskal-Wallis indicates that groups differ, we compare (separately) each \(NB>2\) to the baseline \(NB=2\), and each \(NB>3\) to the baseline \(NB=3\) (we do \emph{not} compare against \(NB=1\)).
  For each pair, the test ranks the pooled observations and computes a statistic from the sum of ranks of one group; the \(p\)-value tells us whether the two groups are drawn from the same distribution.
  We apply \textbf{Holm correction} within each baseline (five comparisons for vs.\ \(NB=2\), four for vs.\ \(NB=3\)) to control the family-wise error rate.
\end{itemize}
So, in short: Kruskal-Wallis tells us ``there is some difference among \(NB\)''; Mann-Whitney with Holm tells us ``which of the larger \(NB\) are significantly different from \(NB=2\) and from \(NB=3\)'' for each metric (see \cref{tab:impl:step3:stats}).

\textit{How to read the reported \(p\)-values.}
In the tables we report \(p\)-values for each test and comparison.
Values below a conventional threshold (e.g.\ \(p<0.05\) after Holm correction) are interpreted as \emph{statistically significant} differences between neighbourhood sizes for that specific metric; values above the threshold mean that the data do not provide enough evidence to distinguish the corresponding \(NB\) from the baseline.
Significance does not say how \emph{large} or \emph{important} the effect is in practice, so we always interpret \(p\)-values together with effect patterns in the figures (e.g.\ metric trends, bad-rates, and violin plots) rather than in isolation.

\textit{Why non-parametric.}
Parametric tests (e.g.\ one-way ANOVA and \(t\)-tests) assume that the metric values are drawn from a normal (Gaussian) distribution, or at least that the sample means are approximately normal by the central limit theorem.
In our setting, the per-simulation metrics (e.g.\ KL divergence, Chi-square) often have \emph{heavy-tailed} distributions: a few simulations can yield very large values, and the distributions are not obviously Gaussian.
Under these conditions, parametric tests can be anticonservative (too many false positives) or have unreliable \(p\)-values.
Non-parametric tests avoid distributional assumptions by working on \emph{ranks} instead of raw values: they only use the ordering of the data, so they are valid for any continuous distribution and are robust to outliers and skewness.
That is why we report Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U rather than ANOVA and \(t\)-tests.

The statistical results (tables and interpretation) are reported in \cref{sec:results:step3:stats}.

\section{Curriculum learning experiment}
\label{sec:impl:curriculum}
%----------------------------------------------------------

Step 3 provides a controlled protocol to compare neighbourhood sizes on trajectories of length 100.
We then designed a second experiment that targets a longer-horizon regime (500-step trajectories) using curriculum learning.
This section describes the dataset, the curriculum schedule, and the evaluation protocol; results (figures, tables, and interpretation) are reported in \cref{sec:results:stochasticity}.
We also explain an important distinction (\emph{teacher forcing} during training vs.\ \emph{free rollouts} at evaluation), because it shapes how training loss relates to long-horizon rollout behaviour.

\subsection{Why we ran this experiment}
\label{sec:impl:curriculum:rationale}

Step 3 uses 300 trajectories of 100 steps and evaluates models at multiple rollout horizons (25, 50, and 100 steps).
That setting is designed for interpretability, but it does not directly address whether the same comparison remains informative when both training data and evaluation extend to a much longer horizon.
We therefore ran a separate experiment on 300 trajectories of 500 time steps each, using a three-phase curriculum (short windows first, then longer ones).
The goal is to test neighbourhood-size choices in a long-horizon regime relevant to tissue regeneration, while keeping the evaluation protocol aligned with free-rollout usage.
We use the same six biological metrics as in Step 3 and evaluate at 25, 100, and 500 steps to probe how errors evolve with rollout length.

\subsection{Data and train/validation split}
\label{sec:impl:curriculum:data}

We use \textbf{300 simulated tissue-growth trajectories}, each of \textbf{500 time steps}, from the same complex tissue model as elsewhere in this thesis (see \cref{sec:impl:data}).
Each trajectory is a sequence of 2D grids of cell types (one-hot encoded for training).
We split the 300 histories into 80\% for training (240 trajectories) and 20\% for validation (60 trajectories), and we keep this split fixed.
Training is done only on the 240 training histories; when we report results, we run evaluation on all 300 (training and validation together) so that the comparison across neighbourhood sizes and step lengths is fair and on the same set of initial conditions.
Before training we check that the loaded histories contain no NaN or Inf, to avoid numerical issues.

\subsection{Curriculum schedule and training protocol}
\label{sec:impl:curriculum:schedule}

We train in \textbf{three phases}, each with a longer time window.
The idea is to start with short windows (cheap to train) and then move to longer ones, so the model first learns short-term dynamics and then refines them on 500-step windows without paying the full cost of long backpropagation from the very beginning.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Phase 1:} windows of 35 steps, 250 epochs, learning rate \(0.0005\), update every 1 step. The loss is computed at every step inside the window.
\item \textbf{Phase 2:} windows of 100 steps, 150 epochs, learning rate \(0.0005\), update every 1 step.
\item \textbf{Phase 3:} windows of 500 steps, 90 epochs, learning rate \(0.0001\), update every 2 steps.
\end{itemize}

In each phase we use early stopping on the training loss (we stop if there is no improvement for 20 epochs and then restore the best weights of that phase), following standard practice for neural network training~\cite{prechelt1998early}.
The loss is mean squared error on the one-hot state; we compute it at every step in the window, not only at the last step, so the model gets a learning signal from all intermediate states.
We train only the \textbf{Mixture NCA} and the \textbf{Stochastic Mixture NCA}; we skip the Standard NCA and the NCA with noise.
For each neighbourhood size we save a checkpoint at the end of each phase and a final checkpoint that we use for all evaluation below.

We do \emph{not} compute a validation loss during training: the 60 validation histories are never used in the training loop.
We only use the training loss for early stopping.
So we cannot see a train-validation gap to detect overfitting; we keep the pipeline simple and avoid an extra pass over the validation set at every epoch.
After training we judge the model by running the evaluation protocol (free rollouts at 25, 100, and 500 steps on all 300 histories and computing the six biological metrics); those metrics are not used to choose or stop the model, only to report and compare.

\subsection{Teacher forcing vs.\ free rollout: what changes and why it matters}
\label{sec:impl:curriculum:teacher_vs_free}

During training we use \emph{teacher forcing}.
That means: when we compute the loss for a window of, say, 35 steps, we feed the model the \emph{true} state from the dataset at each step.
Step 0: we give the model the true initial state \(x_0\), it predicts \(\hat{x}_1\).
Step 1: we give the model the \emph{true} \(x_1\) (not \(\hat{x}_1\)), it predicts \(\hat{x}_2\).
And so on.
So at every step the model sees the correct previous state; its errors are not fed back.
The loss is the mismatch between its predictions and the true next states.
We optimise step-by-step: each optimizer update uses the loss from one step (phases 1--2, with \texttt{update\_every}=1) or from a short unroll of 2 steps (phase 3, with \texttt{update\_every}=2).
We do \emph{not} backpropagate through the whole window; gradients flow through at most 1 or 2 steps per update.
This is efficient and gives a strong learning signal, but the model never practices recovering from its own mistakes.

During evaluation we use \emph{free rollouts} (no teacher forcing).
We take an initial state \(x_0\) from the dataset and we run the model for 25, 100, or 500 steps.
Step 0: we give \(x_0\), the model outputs \(\hat{x}_1\).
Step 1: we give \emph{\(\hat{x}_1\)} (the model's own prediction), it outputs \(\hat{x}_2\).
Step 2: we give \(\hat{x}_2\), and so on.
So the model never sees the true state again after \(x_0\); it only sees its own predictions.
If it makes a small error at step 10, that error is the input for step 11, and the error can grow over time.
After hundreds of steps, the generated tissue can look quite different from the ground truth even if the training loss was low.

The difference between the two regimes is intentional.
At evaluation we want to answer: given only an initial condition, as in real use, can the model produce a good trajectory?
We do not have access to the true states along the way, so the only meaningful test is to run the model on its own; that is exactly what a free rollout does.
Using teacher forcing at evaluation would mean feeding the model the ground truth at every step, which would not reflect any realistic scenario and would make the metrics overly optimistic.

During training, we could in principle also use free rollouts: unroll the model for many steps, feed it its own predictions, and backpropagate through the whole trajectory.
That would train the model in the same regime as evaluation and could reduce the gap.
In practice, however, training with long free rollouts is more expensive and less stable: gradients must flow through hundreds of steps, and optimisation becomes harder.
Teacher forcing avoids that by giving a clean, step-by-step signal and is the standard choice for sequence and trajectory models~\cite{goodfellow2016deep,williams1989learning}.
The downside is that the model never learns to correct its own errors; this train-eval mismatch is often called \emph{exposure bias}~\cite{exposurebias_wang2020}.
A possible compromise is \emph{scheduled teacher forcing}: start with full teacher forcing and gradually feed the true state less often during training, so the model learns to cope with its own predictions~\cite{bengio2015scheduled_sampling}.
We do not use that here; we keep the pipeline simple and accept that part of the gap at long times comes from this design choice.
When we report metrics and show qualitative figures, we always use free rollouts, so the numbers and images reflect the real use case.

\subsection{Models and evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:impl:curriculum:models}

The model architectures (Mixture NCA and Stochastic Mixture NCA) are introduced in \cref{sec:sota:mnca}.
Here we use the same setup as elsewhere in the thesis: extended neighbourhood, 6 state channels (one per cell type), 5 mixture rules, hidden size 128.
We train and evaluate only for \(NB \in \{2, 3, 5\}\) to keep the experiment manageable while still covering a range of neighbourhood sizes.

At evaluation we run \textbf{free rollouts} (as just described): from each initial state we unroll the model for 25, 100, or 500 steps with no teacher forcing, and we compute the six biological metrics (KL divergence, Chi-square, categorical MMD, tumour size difference, border size difference, spatial variance difference) by comparing the \emph{final} generated state to the ground-truth state at the same step.
For the Stochastic Mixture NCA we use hard rule sampling (multinomial sampling at each step), and we run 10 rollouts per initial condition (different random seeds) and report the mean and standard deviation of the metrics.
We use the same sampling and temperature choices as in Step 3 so that the results are comparable.

\section{BBBC031 microscopy segmentation experiment}
\label{sec:impl:bbbc031}
%----------------------------------------------------------

To complement the synthetic tissue experiments, we ran a separate experiment on the public benchmark BBBC031, a synthetic cell-imaging dataset (SIMCEP-generated) for segmentation, where the model grows a mask from seed points toward a ground-truth target.
This section describes the dataset and the experimental protocol (model setup, training schedules, and evaluation metrics).
All numerical results, figures, and comparisons are reported in \cref{sec:results:bbbc031}.

\subsection{The BBBC031 dataset}
\label{sec:impl:bbbc031:dataset}

BBBC031 is a publicly available dataset from the Broad Bioimage Benchmark Collection~\cite{ljosa2012annotated,bbbc031_ref} (BBBC).
It is a \emph{synthetic} dataset: images were generated with a customised version of SIMCEP~\cite{lehmussola2007simcep}, simulating a 24-well plate with phenotypic variation (cell shape and appearance).
The dataset provides cell centroids and, for a subset of images, full cell masks (e.g.\ CELLMASK).
The mask images are in RGBA format: the alpha channel (or the composite of RGB and alpha) indicates the segmented cell regions.
We use these cell-mask images as the \emph{target} for the NCA: the model is trained to grow a segmentation from a small set of seed points (the annotated centroids) until it matches the ground-truth mask, and we evaluate performance with standard segmentation metrics (IoU, Dice) as recommended in recent validation guidelines~\cite{maierhein2024metrics}.

This task is conceptually similar to the synthetic tissue experiments (the model must learn a local update rule that, when iterated from an initial state (seeds on a grid), produces a desired spatial pattern), but the target is a synthetic cell-mask image (with realistic appearance) rather than a simulated tissue history.
NCA-based segmentation has been applied to medical imaging (e.g.\ breast tumour segmentation)~\cite{nca_jeandelle2024_breast}.
It therefore serves as a sanity check and a bridge toward image-based segmentation benchmarks: if the same Stochastic Mixture NCA framework can learn to segment cells from seeds on BBBC031, it supports the generality of the approach; at the same time, the fixed image resolution (we resize to \(96\times96\)) and the small number of images used keep the experiment manageable and reproducible.

\subsection{Motivation for the experiment}
\label{sec:impl:bbbc031:motivation}

The main thesis experiments use a synthetic tissue simulator and focus on the effect of neighbourhood size (\(NB\)) and curriculum design on long-horizon behaviour.
We wanted to test whether the same MNCA architecture and training pipeline could be applied in a different setting: \emph{image-based segmentation} from seed points, with a single static target image per training run (rather than a time series of grids).
The goals were:
\begin{itemize}
  \item to verify that the extended Stochastic Mixture NCA (with configurable neighbourhood size) can be trained on a public (synthetic) cell-imaging benchmark;
  \item to compare two neighbourhood sizes, \(NB=3\) and \(NB=7\), under the same optimisation schedule, and to observe whether the loss and segmentation quality behave similarly to what we saw in the synthetic experiments (where \(NB\ge2\) helped and very small \(NB\) was insufficient);
  \item to produce quantitative metrics (IoU, Dice, MSE on the predicted vs.\ ground-truth mask) suitable for the thesis and for comparison with the synthetic results.
\end{itemize}

The training protocol mirrors the one used in the reference MNCA microscopy setup: we train on one image at a time (one cell-mask target per run), with a pool of states and random rollout lengths between 10 and 20 steps per optimisation step; we run 4000 optimisation steps (gradient updates) with Adam~\cite{kingma2014adam} and a multi-step learning-rate schedule.
At inference we run the model for a fixed number of \emph{simulation} steps (20) from the seed state to obtain the final predicted mask; this is the number that appears in the comparison figures (e.g., ``MNCA after 20 steps'').
It is important to distinguish \textbf{4000 training steps} (number of weight updates) from \textbf{20 simulation steps} (number of NCA updates used to generate the output image): the former is the length of optimisation; the latter is the length of the rollout at evaluation.

\subsection{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:impl:bbbc031:setup}

We selected five images from BBBC031 that have corresponding cell-mask annotations (the first five such images in the ground-truth annotations).
For each image we trained two models: one with neighbourhood size \(NB=3\) and one with \(NB=7\), using the Stochastic Mixture NCA (Extended Mixture NCA with noise), with the same hyperparameters (learning rate \(10^{-3}\), milestones at 1500 and 3000 steps, target size \(96\times96\), 24 state channels, 5 rules, hidden dimension 128).
Each run produces a saved checkpoint and a loss history (one scalar per optimisation step).
After training, we run inference with 20 simulation steps, compare the predicted RGBA output to the ground-truth mask, and compute IoU (intersection over union) and Dice coefficient on the binarized alpha channel (threshold 0.5), and MSE on the full RGBA channels (same as the training loss), in line with common practice in medical image segmentation where Dice is widely used as an overlap metric~\cite{milletari2016v,maierhein2024metrics}.
The metrics are aggregated in a table for the thesis (\cref{tab:bbbc031_metrics}).
\textbf{Evaluation is in-sample:} each model is evaluated on the \emph{same} image it was trained on; we do not use a held-out test set.
The reported metrics therefore reflect how well each run fits its target image and do not assess generalisation to new microscopy images; the comparison between \(NB=3\) and \(NB=7\) is a comparison of optimisation difficulty under the same protocol, not of generalisable segmentation performance.

It is important to note that IoU and Dice are computed only on the \emph{alpha channel} after binarisation: they measure how well the predicted \emph{segmentation mask} (the set of pixels where the model predicts ``cell'') overlaps with the ground-truth mask.
They do not measure whether the RGB channels of the prediction match the ground truth.
The qualitative figures in this section show the full RGBA output (RGB composited with alpha for display), so the \emph{colours} and intensities in the prediction can differ from the ground truth even when IoU and Dice are high: the model may have learned the correct \emph{where} (mask shape) but not the exact \emph{appearance} (channel-wise intensities).
MSE, by contrast, is computed over all four channels and therefore reflects pixel-level fidelity; when MSE remains high while IoU and Dice improve, the overlap of the mask has improved but the predicted pixel values still differ from the target (e.g.\ in colour or intensity).
We train with MSE because it is differentiable and well suited to optimizing over RGBA; we report IoU and Dice because they are the standard metrics for segmentation overlap. It is therefore normal to see high IoU and Dice together with high MSE: the mask shape can be correct (good overlap) while the predicted pixel values still differ from the target.
This distinction explains why some predictions can show high overlap metrics yet look visually different from the ground truth in the side-by-side figures.

For the same reason, the \emph{visual} comparison between the original \(NB=7\) run and the re-trained run can seem at odds with the table: the re-trained run has higher IoU and Dice (better mask overlap), but sometimes the original run can have \emph{lower} MSE on the RGB channels alone (MSE\_RGB), i.e.\ the predicted colours are closer to the ground truth even though the predicted mask overlaps less.
In that case the eye, which is sensitive to colour and intensity, may judge one output as ``closer'' to the target even when the overlap of the segmentation mask is worse.
We therefore report both MSE on the full RGBA output and MSE on the RGB channels only (MSE\_RGB), alongside IoU and Dice on the binarised alpha channel: IoU and Dice quantify segmentation overlap, while MSE/MSE\_RGB quantify pixel-level fidelity.

\subsection{Training schedules explored}
\label{sec:impl:bbbc031:schedules}

We train one model per image and neighbourhood size.
To study the effect of neighbourhood size and the sensitivity of optimisation to training schedules, we consider multiple runs for \(NB=7\) in addition to the \(NB=3\) reference.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\(NB=3\) (reference):} learning rate \(10^{-3}\), 4000 optimisation steps, milestones at 1500 and 3000.
  \item \textbf{\(NB=7\) (original schedule):} same schedule as \(NB=3\) (learning rate \(10^{-3}\), 4000 steps, milestones 1500/3000).
  \item \textbf{\(NB=7\) (re-training schedule):} learning rate \(3\times10^{-4}\), 6000 steps, milestones at 2500 and 4500.
  \item \textbf{\(NB=7\) (extended schedule):} learning rate \(3\times10^{-4}\), 12\,000 steps, milestones at 4500, 6000, 7500, and 9500.
\end{itemize}

All runs use the same architecture and preprocessing described above and are evaluated by running a 20-step rollout from the seed state and computing IoU/Dice/MSE against the ground-truth mask (details and results in \cref{sec:results:bbbc031}).

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:impl:threats}
%----------------------------------------------------------

The conclusions of this chapter are subject to several limitations that are worth stating explicitly.
First, all experiments rely on a \emph{synthetic} tissue simulator with fixed parameters (cell types, differentiation rules, grid size); the effect of neighbourhood size may depend on these choices and may not transfer unchanged to real biological data or to other toy models.
Second, the datasets used are of moderate size (300 trajectories of 100 or 500 steps); larger or more diverse datasets could alter the relative performance of different \(NB\) values.
Third, the neighbourhood size is varied only in the range \(NB \in \{1,\dots,7\}\) (or \(\{2,3,5\}\) in the curriculum experiment); we do not claim that the observed trade-offs extend to much larger receptive fields.
Fourth, in the curriculum experiment we do not compute a validation loss or use free-rollout-based early stopping, so we cannot quantify overfitting; the reported metrics are purely for comparison across configurations, not for model selection.
Acknowledging these limitations helps interpret the results and suggests directions for more generalizable follow-up work (see \cref{sec:conc:future}).
